# Mirror test (first-screen copy)

The right visitor landing on the first screen should feel like they are looking in a mirror: their problem, their world, a clear sign they are in the right place. It is about **words, not design**. From [[wiki/sources/nateherk-10000-ai-websites|Nate Herk, I Studied 10,000 AI Websites]] (pattern 1, [00:25-02:33]).

## Why

AI writes generic copy, so a site can look great and still convert badly. Nate frames it as the [[wiki/concepts/three-ps-pain-person-promise|three P's]] (pain, person, promise) applied to the first thing someone sees, "like the thumbnail or packaging of a YouTube video."

## The three questions the first screen must answer

1. **Who is this for?** Name the visitor, or use their own search word.
2. **What do I get?** The outcome, not the feature ("fill your calendar without answering DMs", not "AI-powered scheduling").
3. **What do I do next?** One button, one action ("book your free call").

Answer these yourself and paste them into Claude **before** asking for any design.

## Evidence (third-party case studies quoted by Nate, not shown on screen)

- Truckers Report (a job site for drivers): CXL rebuilt the page around the driver after surveys (half the traffic on phones, a headline with no benefit, a stock handshake photo). The new page converted **79.3% better** than the 12.1% baseline. Creator-cited.
- An unnamed "City Click" headline change ("businesses grow faster online" to "create a web page for your business"): pricing-page click-through **+90%**. Creator-cited.
- Campaign Monitor matching one headline word to the Google search word (design, create, build): **+31.4% signups**. Creator-cited.

Grade: the mechanism is plausible and consistent with the three P's; the numbers are secondhand and unlinked in the video.
