# Three filters for a one-person business

Nate Herk's own restatement of examples from an Anthropic CEO interview answer, turned into three sequential filters for picking what kind of one-person business to build. It is **not an official checklist** [00:01-02:00]. From [[wiki/sources/nateherk-one-person-business-claude|Nate Herk's one-person business video]].

1. **It owns and deploys its own capital.** Examples given: a proprietary trading firm, or the same model applied to real-estate flipping or a used-car lot. The business uses its own money to buy, improve or trade something for more. Benefit: no need for thousands of customers or a sales team. Cost: real financial risk, and expertise and capital up front.
2. **It is software.** Ordinary people can now build useful software solo with Claude Code, and the range of what's buildable is "basically endless". Buildable does not make it a good one-person business, though. A product that needs custom onboarding, constant support or a salesperson on every deal fails the next filter, however useful it is.
3. **Sales and customer support are highly automatable without becoming a bad experience for users.** The offer has to be repeatable, need little customization, and work without heavy regulation or a custom consultation, because an AI agent has to be able to answer the support questions it generates. This filter ruled out a scheduling tool (too easy, mature market) and an outreach tool (easy to explain, unproven conversion) in favor of the source's actual pick.

## How the filters were used, in order

Filter 1 ruled out the capital-heavy route (no money to risk). Filter 2 pointed at software, given the buildable range. Filter 3 narrowed the software options to something that could run "without hiring a massive team. Or, I guess a team at all." [02:14-02:20] The three ideas that reached a live comparison (scheduling tool, outreach-drafting tool, agent stress-testing tool) then went through a Claude "war room" debate on who would pay, whether software alone could deliver the result, and whether one person could realistically sell and support it. [[wiki/concepts/one-person-business-three-part-architecture|The three-part architecture]] shows what the winning idea became.

## Caveat

These filters are Nate's own translation of an interview answer, not a published Anthropic framework. Use the three-filter shape as a lens for the "what should I build" question, not as a claim about what Dario Amodei formally endorsed.

## Counterpoints from the same creator

- [[wiki/sources/nateherk-ai-businesses-ranked|Nate Herk's later ranking video]] **partly contradicts** filter 2. It ranks AI SaaS last of 13 (hardest to start; 560K new apps in the first half of the year with downloads flat) and puts judgment-selling services (consulting, chief AI officer) at the top. The filters answer "can one person run it"; the ranking asks "is it a good business". See [[wiki/concepts/ai-business-ranking-criteria|AI business ranking criteria]] and [[wiki/concepts/sell-judgment-not-the-build|Sell judgment, not the build]].
- [[wiki/sources/nateherk-4-claude-systems-that-sell|4 Claude Systems That Sell]] sits on the other side of filters 2 and 3. It sells custom builds to clients, which by these filters is a consultancy, not a one-person software business: filter 3 fails, because each sale needs scoping, change management and a pitch. Read the two together as the product path and the services path.
