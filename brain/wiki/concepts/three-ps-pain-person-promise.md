# Three P's: pain, person, promise

Nate Herk's shorthand for what he calls the "AI monetization readiness assessment". Before an AI agent can design a website that doesn't feel generic, it needs to know three things about the business. First given in [[wiki/sources/nateherk-fable-5-1-kills-website-slop|Nate Herk, Fable 5.1 kills website slop]].

## The three questions

1. **Pain**: what pain does this business actually solve?
2. **Person**: who specifically is it solving that pain for?
3. **Promise**: what is the specific promise of how the product or service solves that pain, for that person?

It is the second of his seven rules for non-slop websites, right after "have brand guidelines" and before anything about scroll or visual treatment.

## A design input as well as a marketing input

Nate ties visual and interaction decisions straight back to the three P's. [[wiki/concepts/scroll-feedback|Scroll feedback]] intensity ("it depends on the business… it depends on the three P's") is the clearest example: the site's person decides how much motion reads as premium and how much reads as try-hard. Until pain, person and promise are pinned down, the other six rules have nothing to calibrate against. Styling decisions made in the abstract, before the business and its audience are known, are how [[wiki/concepts/ai-website-slop|AI website slop]] happens.

## The worked example: business positioning

[[wiki/sources/nateherk-one-person-business-claude|A later Nate Herk video]] applies the same framework to business positioning. For his live-built Agent Report Card:

- pain = "agencies are manually testing customer support agents and can't prove the quality of them before pushing them into production"
- person = "an AI automation agency who is deploying customer support agents for their clients"
- promise = "Agent Report Card runs your agents through 16 or more high-risk scenarios and shows you exactly where those agents fail and creates a client-ready report."

Two things this adds:

- **The order is deliberate.** Pain, person, promise: you have to know exactly who you are solving the pain for before you can make a specific promise.
- **The three P's drive [[wiki/techniques/narrow-the-niche-before-general|narrowing the niche]].** A vague person ("anyone using AI agents") forces a vague promise ("we test your agents"). Narrowing the person to "an AI agency deploying customer-support agents" makes the promise specific enough to be a product claim instead of a slogan.

## Other applications

- [[wiki/sources/nateherk-10000-ai-websites|Nate Herk, 10,000 AI Websites]] restates the three P's as the [[wiki/concepts/mirror-test-first-screen|mirror test]] for the first screen: who is this for, what do I get, what do I do next.
- [[wiki/sources/nateherk-ai-businesses-ranked|Every AI Business Ranked]] adds a measurable end to the promise: every offer needs one clear business-outcome metric the client cares about. See [[wiki/concepts/ai-business-ranking-criteria|AI business ranking criteria]].

Same creator, the same framework applied to design register, business positioning and first-screen copy. Treat it as one durable concept.
