# Viewer tier conversion (new, casual, regular)

YouTube Studio's "Audience by watch behavior" panel splits your monthly audience into three tiers: new viewers, casual viewers and regular viewers. The source identifies movement between those tiers, rather than raw view count, as the single biggest driver of [[wiki/concepts/youtube-trust-score|trust score]].

Source: [[wiki/sources/dmscales-shorts-algorithm|dmscales, "The Entire Shorts Algorithm Explained in 180 Seconds"]] (01:50-02:07). The panel is real; its weight in distribution is the creator's claim.

## The claim

Because each short goes to a fresh [[wiki/concepts/seed-audience-testing|seed audience]], a growing channel keeps pouring strangers into the "new viewers" bucket, and the monthly audience number rises on its own. That growth is cheap and means little by itself. What YouTube reportedly weighs heavily is whether those strangers come back: new converting to casual, casual to regular. That is the evidence that spending viewers on you produces lasting watch time rather than a one-off impression.

The example channel on screen is 88.7% new, 11.3% casual and under 0.1% regular, so even a channel doing 71M views in a period can be almost entirely strangers. The on-screen captions say "your trust score goes up significantly" and then "this happens naturally": the creator frames conversion as a result of consistent posting, not something you engineer directly.

## Why it argues for frequent uploads

Conversion needs repeat surface area. A viewer can only become casual if they meet you again soon after the first time. Post weekly and the first impression has faded before the second arrives. That is a more precise reason for a daily cadence than "the algorithm likes consistency": what matters is the interval between exposures, not the count.

## How to read it

Treat the tier split as the channel-health metric and views as the vanity one. A month where views were flat but the casual and regular share rose is a better month than the reverse. Expect the regular tier to sit near zero on a young channel, since it takes months of history to fill.
