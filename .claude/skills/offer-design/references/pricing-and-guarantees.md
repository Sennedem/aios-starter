# Pricing, guarantees, scarcity, naming and tiers

The second half of the `/offer-design` method, summarized from Alex Hormozi's *$100M Offers*.

## Pricing

- **Price to value, not to cost or the hour.** The hour is an input; the buyer pays for the outcome. A service that saves a firm a large sum a year is not priced off the seller's time.
- **Charge a premium on purpose.** A higher price brings more committed buyers, fewer refunds and churn, and more budget to deliver well. Cheap attracts the worst clients. Many new sellers under-price out of caution; when the value is shown and the economics hold, anchor higher than the first instinct.
- **Anchor high, then justify with the stack.** Show the assembled value first so the price lands against a number already in the buyer's head.
- **Free proof cases, then paid.** A seller with no proof can run one or two free cases to create it, then charge. Keep the free case separate from the paid price: it removes risk at launch, it is not the permanent price.
- **Payment plans** lower the felt cost of a large price (two to four payments) without discounting the total. Use them when the price is sound and the lump sum is the objection.

## Guarantees: reverse the risk

Match the guarantee to what the user can actually honor.

| Type | Shape | When to use |
|---|---|---|
| Unconditional | "Don't like it, full refund, no questions." | Strongest trust signal; use when the worst case (a refund) is survivable. Suits low-ticket B2C. |
| Conditional | "Do X, Y and Z; if you don't get the result, refund or we keep working free." | Filters for committed buyers by tying the refund to the buyer doing their part. Suits services. |
| Outcome ("better than money back") | "If you don't hit [result] in [time], you get [refund plus a bonus], or we work until you do." | Highest perceived likelihood; only when the outcome is really in the seller's control. |
| Anti-guarantee | "All sales final," with a strong stated reason. | Rare; premium or bespoke work where a guarantee would attract the wrong buyers. |

Make the guarantee specific and visible: a named result and timeframe beats "satisfaction guaranteed". A guarantee whose delivery would need informal or off-contract work is not a valid design.

## Scarcity and urgency: real only

- **Scarcity:** a real cohort cap, limited client slots, founding-member seats. A solo seller has real capacity limits; say so honestly.
- **Urgency:** a real start date, a founding price that actually closes, an enrolment deadline.
- **Never** fake a countdown or "only 2 left". If the scarcity is not real, create a real one (actually cap the cohort) or drop the lever.

## Bonuses

- Each bonus answers a secondary objection ("what about after the project ends?" gets 30 days of support).
- Add them on top of the core with their own stated value.
- Time-box bonuses to enrolment to add honest urgency.

## MAGIC naming

Generate 3 to 5 options and rank them. A strong offer name is:
- **Magnetic:** pulls the right person in.
- **Aspirational:** points at the dream outcome.
- **Graphic:** concrete ("The 90-Day Launch Sprint", not "Business Programme").
- **Identity-tied:** the buyer wants to be the kind of person who buys it.
- **Catchy:** easy to say and remember.

Naming copy goes through the `writing` skill. Names follow the customer's own words and units (their term, their calendar), not a description written from the seller's side.

## Free to paid tiers

For community, course or info products, set the boundary on purpose:
- **Free tier is the wedge.** It captures leads and raises Perceived Likelihood at almost no effort for the buyer. It must deliver a real standalone win, not a crippled teaser.
- **Paid tier is the transformation:** the structured path to the outcome the free tier makes them want. Price it on that transformation.
- **The bridge:** how the free tier naturally surfaces the paid one (lead magnet, nurture, offer). `sales-engine` funnel mode takes over here.
- Do not give the transformation away free, and do not gate the wedge. Getting this line wrong is the most common productization mistake.
